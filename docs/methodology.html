Methodology — How the matriculation metrics are estimated

This page documents the multi-source weighted estimator used to produce the T25 % (and companion T10 %, T50 %, LAC15 %, UC %) numbers across this site, and explains why our numbers will diverge from other published Bay Area high school rankings.

1. What "T25" means in this dataset

Every T25 % on this site refers to:

  • Universe: U.S. News & World Report Top-25 National Universities, list current as of the 2024–2025 ranking cycle. Ties at the boundary slightly expand the set.
  • Outcome counted: matriculation (where students actually enroll), not admission/acceptance.
  • Excluded: Top Liberal Arts Colleges (Williams, Amherst, Pomona, Swarthmore, etc.), other UC campuses outside T25 (Davis, Irvine, Santa Barbara, Santa Cruz, Riverside, Merced), Stanford-style hybrid programs not in the T25 list.

1b. The four companion metrics: T10 %, T50 %, LAC15 %, UC %

T25 % is one slice of selectivity. To give a fuller picture without abandoning the simplicity of single-number comparison, the schools tables now include four companion metrics. Each is built on the same matriculation-not-acceptance basis as T25 %, and each can be sorted independently in the tables.

Dedicated estimation pages: How T10 % is estimated · How T50 % is estimated · How LAC15 % is estimated — each includes the M-anchor measured values, worked examples, ratio tables, and known sources of error per metric.

2. Why our numbers differ from chicardgo and similar sites

Important: chicardgo.com publishes a "T25 %" that is not a pure T25 matriculation rate. Per their published methodology:
chicardgo "T25 %" = 45% × (T25 National matriculation) + 25% × (T15 Liberal Arts matriculation) + 10% × (other UC system) + 20% × (College Readiness Score)
This is a composite index that mixes T25 nationals, top LACs, all UCs, and a readiness score. Their published numbers will be ~15–20 percentage points higher than ours for the same school. The difference is a definitional gap, not a data error.

Other sources have their own biases:

  • findingschool.com tends to under-report (their methodology applies strict matriculation discounting).
  • Niche.com uses student/alumni surveys — can lag and skew optimistic.
  • Aggregator blogs (Sierra Admissions, College Transitions) often summarize rankings without publishing hard matriculation numbers.
  • School profile PDFs may publish acceptance lists rather than matriculation lists; acceptance counts overstate matriculation by 30–50% for top schools.

3. Source hierarchy and weights

For each school, every available data point is assigned to a tier. The final estimate is a weighted average, with higher-weight tiers dominating when sources conflict.

4. The estimator

For each school s:
  T25%(s) = Σi ( sourcei.value × sourcei.weight ) / Σi sourcei.weight

Confidence tag is set by the highest-tier source available:
  Tier 1 only → M (measured)
  Tiers 1–2 with multiple agreement → M
  Tiers 2–4 → E (estimated)
  Tiers 4–6 only → R (rough)
  No tier-1 to tier-5 data → N (no data, floor estimate)

5. Why not a real ML model

The user-facing question — "can you train a model to improve accuracy?" — has a short honest answer: no, the dataset doesn't support it.

A real regression model needs labeled training data. Here, "labeled" means a school where we know the true T25 % with certainty. That's the M-tagged anchor set: roughly 8–10 schools (Gunn, Paly, Carlmont, Harker, Menlo, Nueva, College Prep, Marin Academy, Urban, plus Lynbrook now Tier 1). Eight to ten labeled examples cannot train a meaningful regression that generalizes to ~150 schools, especially when the predictive features (test scores, demographics, district, religious affiliation) are themselves noisy.

The weighted estimator above is the honest version of "more accurate." It's transparent about which source contributed what, and the weights are decisions you can audit and override. A black-box ML model on this size of dataset would just be a less-honest version of the same heuristic.

6. What would actually improve accuracy

In rough order of impact, more data of these types would meaningfully shift the estimates:

  1. UC source-school CSV pulled systematically per year — would upgrade ~30 public schools from R to E with hard UCB+UCLA+UCSD numbers. The dashboard is interactive and not WebFetch-friendly; would require a person to manually pull or a Selenium scrape.
  2. Direct outreach to school counseling offices — many schools have detailed per-college matriculation data internally that they don't publish online but will share on request.
  3. Naviance / SCOIR exports — most Bay Area private schools and many publics use these tools internally. A current student or parent at the school can typically see aggregated outcomes.
  4. LinkedIn alumni searches — for each school, search current students at each T25. Tedious and rate-limited but produces hard counts. Best for schools with no other public data.

8. Acceptance vs. matriculation conversion

Many schools (especially Catholic college-preps and some independents like BASIS Independent Fremont, Bay School of SF, Bentley) publish acceptance lists rather than matriculation lists. Converting one to the other is the single most error-prone step in this dataset. The formal rule:

TN_matriculation = TN_acceptance × yield(N)

One yield multiplier per tier. No stacking. Earlier drafts of the estimator stacked a yield factor and a separate selection factor, which compressed each tier independently and broke internal consistency.

The subset rule

For every school, the following must hold on both acceptance and matriculation counts:

T10 ≤ T15 ≤ T25 ≤ T50 ≤ T100

This is definitional — T15 is a strict superset of T10, and so on. Any row that violates this ordering is a methodology error, not a real-world finding.

Worked counterexample — BASIS Independent Fremont (before fix). School publishes acceptance counts only. Stacked-discount conversion took 29% T15 acceptance → ~16% T15 matriculation, then estimated T25 matriculation at ~22% — implying T25 < T15, which is impossible. Switching to a single yield-per-tier multiplier produced the corrected T25 estimate of ~32%, with T15 ≤ T25 properly preserved.

Default yield factors by tier

Use these as defaults when converting acceptance counts to matriculation. Yield declines as the bucket broadens because higher-tier admits cross-admit each other heavily and pick the most-selective option, while lower-tier admits are more diffusely distributed across the bucket. If the school publishes both acceptance and matriculation for any tier, prefer the measured ratio.

TierDefault yieldReasoning
T100.85Cross-admit pool concentrated; admits typically pick the single most-selective option.
T150.85Same as T10 — adding 5 more elite schools doesn't materially change yield behavior.
T250.80Bucket starts to include large publics (UCB/UCLA/UMich/UVA) where yield is lower.
T500.70Many cross-admits trade down to in-state public or merit-aid school outside T50.
T1000.60Broad bucket; substantial leakage to LACs, military, conservatories, in-state.

Three sanity checks for every new school estimate

  1. Subset check. Does T10 ≤ T15 ≤ T25 ≤ T50 hold? If not, the conversion is wrong — fix before publishing.
  2. Yield-stacking check. Did you apply more than one discount factor between acceptance and matriculation? If yes, collapse to a single yield per tier.
  3. Plausibility check. Does T25 % land in the typical range for the school's peer band (see Section 1b ratio table)? A 2× outlier from peers usually means an acceptance list was read as a matriculation list.

Yield is school-type dependent

The default yield table above (T10 0.85, T15 0.85, T25 0.80, etc.) is a starting point. The T25 yield in particular varies meaningfully by school type because yield is a behavioral parameter of the student population, not a fixed property of the T25 bucket. 9. Cross-check vs chicardgo (and the residual gap)

chicardgo.com is the most widely cited Bay Area high school ranking, and its T25 % numbers run materially higher than ours for the same schools. This section decomposes that gap honestly: part of it is a definitional gap (chicardgo's "T25 %" is a composite that includes T15 LACs and other UCs, which we exclude by definition), and part of it is a methodological gap (their formula does not separate acceptance from matriculation, and applies a yield assumption that is too low for top-of-funnel schools).

Reading the decomposition. When "ours + LAC15 + other-UC" is roughly equal to chicardgo, the entire gap is definitional — they are measuring a broader bucket. When it exceeds chicardgo, our pure T25 number is actually higher on a like-for-like basis, and chicardgo's index is being held back by their college-readiness weighting (20% of their composite). When it is less than chicardgo (Stanford OHS, Harker), the residual is the methodological gap: chicardgo conflates acceptance and matriculation and uses an implied yield closer to 100%, which inflates schools where the school publishes acceptance lists but the actual yield is meaningfully below 100%.

Honest closing. chicardgo's numbers are not wrong — they are a different metric. They measure "elite college outcomes broadly" by combining T25 nationals, top LACs, all UCs, and a college-readiness signal. We measure pure T25 matriculation (where students enroll at U.S. News Top-25 National Universities only). The two metrics rank the schools in roughly the same order — the absolute scale is different. If you want a single broader number, mentally add ~10-15pp to our T25 % for elite-feeding privates and ~5-10pp for top publics; that approximates the chicardgo "T25 %." If you want pure prestige-selectivity, use ours.
Bottom line: Use the rankings as a starting point, not an answer. The single biggest source of variance in any individual student's outcome is their own aptitude and effort — high-quality schools amplify that, but they don't replace it. The "best high school to attend" is the one whose culture, peer group, distance, and cost fit your family — matriculation rankings just narrow the field.
